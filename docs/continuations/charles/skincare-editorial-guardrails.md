# Downs Style skincare editorial guardrails

What we can say, what needs support, and what should stay out of copy.

> U.S.-focused working guide for blog posts, captions, newsletters, and social content. This is practical editorial guidance, not legal advice.

## The operating rule

Write what we can verify, describe personal reactions as personal reactions, disclose every material brand relationship, and do not turn a cosmetic review into a medical promise. The FTC evaluates the overall impression of an ad—not only isolated words—and expects objective claims to be truthful, not misleading, and supported before publication. FDA rules also draw a line between appearance or cleansing claims and claims that a product treats disease or affects the structure or function of the body.

- Personal opinion is welcome: scent, texture, packaging, ritual, and preference are the heart of the Downs Style voice.
- A factual claim is still factual when introduced with “we think,” “it is known for,” or “it is said to.” Those phrases do not replace evidence.
- Use the current product label or current brand page for names and ingredients. Ingredient lists and formulas change.
- Never describe a result or product experience that did not actually happen.

## Usually safe to say

- Sensory observation: “The texture is rich and bouncy,” “the scent is distinctly floral,” or “the mist feels light.”
- Personal preference: “We reach for this when we want a quick refresh,” or “we prefer the richer texture.”
- Neutral formula facts from a current source: “The ingredient list includes rosewater, aloe, Red 40, and Yellow 5.”
- Attributed brand facts: “The brand says the roses are steam-distilled in the South of France.” Link the source when the detail matters.
- Honest drawbacks: fragrance, essential oils, dyes, packaging, texture, or a formula that may not match a reader’s stated preference.
- Appearance-focused language tied to an actual observation: “Our skin looked dewier” is safer than “this transforms dehydrated skin.”

## Say only with support and careful wording

- Performance terms such as “hydrating,” “soothing,” “brightening,” “firming,” “clinically proven,” or “dermatologist tested.” Confirm what was tested, by whom, on how many people, for how long, and whether the result matches the exact statement.
- Ingredient-effect explanations. Prefer an attributed, narrow sentence—“the brand describes glycerin as a humectant”—over a guaranteed product outcome.
- “For sensitive skin,” “hypoallergenic,” “fragrance-free,” and “non-comedogenic.” These terms do not guarantee universal tolerance; FDA notes that some have no federal definition. Attribute the claim and never turn it into “safe for sensitive skin.”
- “Natural,” “all natural,” “clean,” “organic,” or “non-toxic.” Use only when the wording is accurate, narrowly defined, and substantiated. An organic claim may also need to match USDA certification rules.
- Comparisons such as “best,” “safer,” “better,” or “more effective.” State the basis and avoid sweeping superiority claims.
- Statistics, study findings, before-and-after language, and typical-results claims. Preserve the study’s limits and do not make an exceptional result sound typical.

## Do not say

- That an ordinary cosmetic treats, cures, prevents, or mitigates acne, eczema, rosacea, dermatitis, infection, inflammation, or another disease.
- That a product “heals,” “regenerates cells,” “stimulates collagen,” “repairs the skin barrier,” “kills bacteria,” or otherwise changes body structure or function unless the product is lawfully marketed for that drug use and the exact claim is supported.
- “FDA-approved” or “FDA-certified” for an ordinary cosmetic. FDA generally does not pre-approve cosmetics or cosmetic claims, although color additives are a separate category.
- “Chemical-free,” “toxin-free,” “completely safe,” “safe for everyone,” “zero side effects,” or an unqualified “non-toxic.”
- “All natural” when the current ingredient list includes synthetic ingredients, dyes, or other components inconsistent with that absolute claim.
- An endorsement based on a product we did not try, a made-up personal result, an AI-generated testimonial presented as real, or a positive opinion purchased as a condition of compensation.
- A buried or vague disclosure such as “collab,” “sp,” or a disclosure hidden in a footer, About page, or cluster of hashtags.

## Disclosures: when and how

A material connection includes money, free or discounted products, employment, and relevant personal or family relationships. Make the disclosure clear, close to the recommendation, and hard to miss. For a blog post, place it before the first product recommendation or shopping link; repeat it near the relevant product if only one item is connected. Use the same language as the post.

- **Affiliate:** “This post contains affiliate links. If you buy through them, Downs Style may earn a commission at no extra cost to you.”
- **Gifted:** “Gifted by [Brand]. The opinions are our own.”
- **Paid:** “Paid partnership with [Brand].”
- **No material connection:** No disclosure is required merely to say that we bought and liked a product.

A disclosure cannot rescue a false claim. If the endorsement itself is inaccurate or unsupported, rewrite or remove it.

## Rose toners draft: exact house rulings

- **Mario Badescu:** Say that the current formula lists Red 40 and Yellow 5. Keep the disclosure factual and neutral. Do not call the formula “au natural,” “dye-free,” or anti-inflammatory, and do not imply that bladderwrack guarantees a rejuvenating effect.
- **Whamisa:** Correct the spelling to Whamisa. It is fair to describe the aloe base, fermented flower complex, rich texture, and essential-oil bouquet. Do not promise that it is universally suitable for sensitive skin.
- **Santa Maria Novella:** Attribute the documented 1381 history to the brand and describe Damask rose water as an ingredient. Do not recycle historical medicinal uses as present-day efficacy claims.
- **Chantecaille:** The current list is two ingredients—Rose de Mai flower water and lactobacillus ferment. Do not convert brand language about antioxidants or purification into guaranteed outcomes.
- **Sisley-Paris:** Describe the rose, cornflower, and witch-hazel floral waters and disclose that the formula includes fragrance. Do not call it universally soothing or sensitivity-safe.
- **Across the post:** Avoid prices, medical promises, invented results, and universal safety claims. If a shopping link becomes an affiliate link later, add the disclosure before publication.

## Pre-publication check

1. Open every shopping link and confirm the product name, current ingredient list, and spelling.
2. Separate what we experienced, what the label says, and what the brand claims.
3. Search the draft for medical verbs: treat, cure, prevent, heal, repair, regenerate, stimulate, kill, reduce inflammation.
4. Confirm every performance claim has support that matches the exact wording.
5. Add affiliate, gifted, paid, employment, family, or other material-connection disclosures where required.
6. Check that drawbacks are factual rather than safety verdicts.
7. Use only images Charles owns, licensed images, or properly permitted brand assets.
8. Read aloud once for cadence; remove hype, absolutes, filler, and accidental guarantees.
